Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) vision-language models route each token to a few experts, and many proposals promise smarter routers. We test the premise that routing carries exploitable signal, on two frozen backbones and one 8 GB GPU, with DecisionMoE: a router that ranks experts with TOPSIS and lets a PPO policy choose criterion weights and the number of active experts K per step, compared with the gate's Top-K at matched, token-weighted expert FLOPs. (i) On a sparsely upcycled model (MoE-LLaVA-Phi2-2.7B$\times$4, 8,803 synthetic questions, POPE) accuracy is insensitive to expert selection: Top-K for K=1..4, random routing, single experts and fixed MCDM routing all fall in a 2.5-point band, and a learned policy only rediscovers K=1. (ii) On a model with 64 natively trained experts (DeepSeek-VL2-Tiny) the picture reverses: random experts give 0.000 accuracy and dropping the best expert costs 8.8 points, yet Top-3 is within 0.8 points of Top-6 at half the FLOPs. TOPSIS re-ranking adds nothing, even with an oracle domain label. (iii) Adaptive K helps only with long generation. With brief chain-of-thought on AI2D and ScienceQA-IMG, retrained PPO policies beat the matched-FLOPs Top-K mixture by about 1--3 points at $\lambda$=0.4 (positive in all eight seeds, CIs excluding zero in six), but the gain is task-specific (AI2D policies do not transfer), unstable at stronger cost pressure, and a gate-mass heuristic matches it at one setting. (iv) A step-averaged FLOPs reward is gamed by the policy (reported 0.33, true token-weighted 0.99), and 7.5\% of benchmark references are wrong, with 44\% of flagged hallucinations being broken traps.
Routing matters when experts are diverse, but beyond the gate's Top-K the achievable gain is small; we recommend reporting sensitivity analyses and token-weighted costs.
